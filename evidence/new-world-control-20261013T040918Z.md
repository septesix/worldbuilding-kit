# 01 — The Law of Names

## What counts as a name

A **name** is a word enrolled against **one particular thing**. That is the whole test, and it is a
test of *paper*, not of poetry: if the parish register, the boat-roll, the guild book or the
tollhouse's own Index of Particulars has that word standing alone against one bearer, the word is a
name and the water will take it.

- **Person names** — enrolled at the naming. Always spendable.
- **House, door, street, wharf, field, gate names** — spendable. People spend the names of their own
  houses by accident more often than anything else. (`Abel Quarm, folio 806.`)
- **Boat names** — spendable, and *a boat's bank is the bank it is enrolled at*, not the bank it lies
  against. Half the boat-folk of the Reeds cannot say the names of their own best boats.
- **Animal names** — spendable if enrolled. The Reeds enrol almost nothing, so a Reed dog keeps his
  name and a Quays cat loses hers at the first ill-worded call across the Cut.
- **Gods** — the Index of Particulars declines to hold entries for any of them, on the stated ground
  that no parish can prove it has one enrolled. This is the most dangerous open question in the
  city's law, and the Chapel of the Kept Word and the Silent Ferry have been shouting about it for
  two hundred folios. (You can shout for hours. It is not a name. Probably.)
- **Common nouns** — exempt: `mother`, `woman`, `the ferryman`, `the little one`, `water`, `knife`.
  This exemption is why the speech of this city has grown an enormous, baroque, aching furniture of
  descriptors, and why **Reedward** has forty ways to say *the person I cannot say*.
- **Epithets that have hardened** — spendable. This is called **catching**. A nickname repeated
  enough is held to have become enrolled, and there are clerks whose entire living is arguing whether
  *Long Tomas* has caught. The rich protect themselves by giving their children eleven names, all
  enrolled, and using none of them at each other.

## The right side and the wrong side

Every enrolled thing has a **bank of belonging** — the bank of its hearth, its parish, its enrolment.
Speak the name while standing on the *other* bank's ground and the word is spent.

- Standing on the Quays and saying a Reed-born name: **spent.**
- Standing in the Reeds and saying a Quays-born name: **spent.** (Symmetrical in law. Brutal in
  practice, because all the Quays' names belong to the Quays, and the Quays is where the work is.)

# 02 — The Tollhouse and the Ledger

## The building

The tollhouse stands **on piles, astride the Cut**, in the one place in Sallowcote where you are
simultaneously on both banks and neither. They built it there on purpose, in the first folio, so that
its own clerks could conduct business without spending — and the resulting joke, that *the only
people in this city who can afford to talk are the ones who decide what talking costs*, is written
above the water-gate in letters a foot high, and has never been removed, and no one agrees who put it
there.

Four storeys of brick, one storey of water. Upstream face: the **Assay**. Downstream: the **Marking
Room**. Middle, and structurally absurd: the **First Page**, a vault holding folio 1, which is kept
locked because the first page contains the city's name and there is one mouth alive permitted to say
it.

## The Ledger

- **Format.** Continuous. One folio per year, so the calendar *is* the book: today is
  **folio 812, page 6**. Ages are stated in pages. "He's thirty pages" is how you say a boy is
  thirty. A death is a **closing**.
- **Ordering.** Entries are filed **by speaker**, not by word. This is the founding administrative
  decision, and it is the whole politics of the place in one line: the book is a census of mouths, and
  to look up *what a name costs* you must instead read **every mouth that has ever said it**. There
  is no index of names. There has been a standing petition to build one for three hundred folios. The
  petition is itself a name and someone once spent it.
- **Columns.** `PAGE · SPEAKER · NAME · BANK THEY STOOD ON · BANK IT BELONGS TO · AUTHORITY · MARK`
  — where **AUTHORITY** means *who told the clerk this happened*, which is the column a clever reader
  should always look at, and in `05-ledger.md` you will notice that it does not always contain a
  good answer.

# 03 — The City

## The Cut

A canal dug straight as an accusation, four hundred years before the ledger, through marsh that
wouldn't hold a post. Fifty feet of black tidal water, brick walls, iron rings worn thin by rope. It
is called **the Cut** because that is what a ditch is called in the common tongue, and common nouns
are exempt, and the city's real water-name was lost in folio 2 — there is a page of the ledger with an
entry so old the ink has gone the colour of the water, and no one alive can say what the river was
called, and there are eight hundred years of people pointing at it and saying *the Cut*
like someone patting a horse they are afraid of.

Twice a day the tide stands up in it and everything on both banks smells of silt and iron. Reed
children are told the Cut rises to listen.

## The Quays (north bank)

Brick, guildhalls, eleven parish registers, chains of houses with names over every door — and every
one of those door-names is a small fortune sitting in the wrong place, because a Quays household is a
household that must never be named from a boat.

- **Wards:** Tallow's North, Guildwash, Three Bellmen, **the Stone**, the Register's Quarter,
  Ash House, the Long Market, the Crane, the Shallows (rich, ironically named: the wealthiest houses
  in the city have the most unspeakable gardens).
- **The Long Market** — the source of the whole misery. Everything in the Reeds that must be sold is
  sold here, on Quays ground, by people who belong forty yards south. A market-day in the Reeds is a
  slow hemorrhage of words. Farmers come back at dusk sitting silent in their boats, having lost a
  wife's name, a horse's name, a lane, and the word for their own best cabbage, and the tollhouse
  takes its percentage of the goods and none of the blame.
- **The Crane** — where a Quays ship's cargo is lifted. A crane has a name. There has been a standing
  argument about that crane for sixty folios.

## The Reeds (south bank)

Reed and wattle on pilings, boat-houses, no registers, no door-names, no enrolments: a district that
has survived by **being unprovable**. Nothing in the Reeds is officially a particular. Houses are
pointed at. Children are named on boats. This is not poverty's slovenliness; it is four centuries of
a very clever, very bitter bookkeeping, and every Reed you meet will explain it to you in writing on a
slate, because saying it aloud on the wrong bank would cost them.

# 04 — Estates, or: how a city organises itself around vocabulary

## The four estates

**The Full-mouthed.** Name-rich families of the Quays who hold thousands of unspent words, and spend
them at each other for pleasure. They live in houses with named rooms, and each guest is handed, on
arrival, a **card of permitted words** — the roster of everything in that house you may say without
cost. A Full-mouthed dinner party is a small war: someone always says the wrong thing at the wrong
moment on purpose, and the host's face as the mouth declines is the entertainment. Their children are
taught to speak *slowly*, and their whole accent is a legend of hesitation.

**The Thinned.** Everyone with a working stock of words and no spare. Small guild-folk, clerks,
journeymen. They keep a **spare list** — thirty or forty names they have decided in advance they can
afford to lose, written on paper and read weekly so the counterfeits don't catch. Thinned people
make the city run, and are quietly furious, and are the ones the tollhouse trusts with the pens.

**The Bare.** The poor of both banks, but mostly the Reeds: names gone, mostly. A Bare person lives
in a house with no name, with a spouse they call *my old one*, on a lane they point at, in a city
they cannot say. They are not mute — **the Bare are the most articulate people in Sallowcote**,
because they have had to fill a language with everything but names. Their speech is dense with
kinship-words, landscape-words, verb-music, and forty ways to say *the person I cannot say*. A Bare
ferryman can tell you a thirty-minute story that contains no proper nouns at all, and it will be the
best thing you hear all week.

**The Attainted.** Legal non-persons. Cannot say their own name, therefore cannot claim themselves.
About four hundred of them, concentrated in Otter Row, and served by **the Silent Ferry**: an order
who walk behind the attainted and *say their names for them*, at cost, in the exact tone of someone
giving away money. Silent Ferrymen take a vow never to use a name of their own — which is, in this
city, the most radical religious act anybody has thought of.

# 06 — Hooks, and the seams I left on purpose

## Six ways in

1. **The Hearing of Avelin.** Page 812, and the loudest argument in the city: a Reed girl's name has
   *caught* — the guild book of the Crane says the word is enrolled against a bend in the water, the
   Reeds say it's a common noun, and the Ordinance of One Speech says the Reedward form dies with the
   English one. Everyone who has ever said `Avelin` in the Long Market is thirty days from being Bare.
   The girl is on the bank. She is not in the courtroom, because the courtroom is on the wrong side of
   her, and she will lose her name by being *summoned by it*. Whose side are you on, and can you say
   the word out loud to get in the door?
